One regression was introduced and caught. A migration treated a passage-linked listening question as standalone, which overwrote a question stem and left one item with no correct answer among its options — the only key-not-in-options defect ever present in this bank. Found by a corpus-wide sweep, repaired, and the sweep is now mandatory after every content migration.

Distractor quality — also now closed. 35 items had distractors so weak the item tested nothing: rhyme-fillers (dividend against sediment/condiment/ligament), absurd nouns, mutually eliminable synonym pairs (offering both until and till, so both die at once and a 4-way item becomes a 2-way one), and two article tells — “I work in an _____” where only the key begins with a vowel, making the item answerable with zero English vocabulary. Nothing from the audit remains open. The one number that still looks wrong is deliberate: grammar_vocab reads 29/34 on “the visibly-longest option is the answer”, because those are register items (“Which email is appropriate?”) where the key is the formal option and formal English is intrinsically wordier, plus tense items where the correct form cannot be shorter (will have been working vs will work). Levelling them would destroy the construct being tested.

The limit of this audit

Everything above is judgement from inspection, not evidence from learners

No learner response was ever examined, because almost none exist — 79 attempts from 12 users at the time of writing. So this audit can say an item is well-formed, unambiguous and correctly keyed. It cannot say whether the item actually separates a strong learner from a weak one, which is ultimately the only thing that matters in a test.

Two claims in particular rest on nothing but the original author's judgement: the CEFR labels, which have never been checked against how anyone performs, and the level estimate the app writes onto every profile and reports after a mock exam. If the labels drift, every learner is told something wrong about themselves, and no amount of content review would reveal it. The AI grading of the 72 writing and 108 speaking prompts is likewise entirely unaudited.

What closes this gap: the attempts table already logs everything required, so nothing needs building — only volume. Three queries are committed at analysis/ that turn responses into item evidence: facility and discrimination per item, whether difficulty actually rises with CEFR label, and which wrong answers anyone picks. The alarm worth watching is negative discrimination — an item your best learners get wrong more often than your worst. That has almost no innocent explanation, and it catches automatically the same defect class this audit had to hunt by hand across 1,313 items.

The three findings that matter

1. The correct answer is usually the longest one

This is the most serious defect found, and it is not a content error — it is a pattern across hundreds of items. Ignore the passage, ignore the audio, always pick the longest option:

The pattern scales with level: the higher the band, the more the answer gives itself away. On C1 reading it reaches 90%. The cause is a house item-writing habit — the key is written as a careful, hedged, both-sides statement while the distractors are short absolutes. A test-wise candidate beats your upper levels without any English at all. Deferred by your decision: levelling option lengths across ~194 C1/C2 items is a content round, not a repair.

2. 83% of your speaking prompts are unreachable

You have 108 speaking prompts. Learners can only ever see 18 of them. pickPromptForPhase in practice.speaking.tsx:68 selects with pool.find(p => p.cefr_level === target) — the first array match, not a random one. With exactly 6 prompts per (level, phase) group, five in every six are dead. pickOralForPhase in the mock exam has the same bug.

The query has no ORDER BY, so which prompt survives is arbitrary Postgres row order and can change silently whenever rows are updated. Speaking is the only skill affected — listening, reading and grammar all randomise over a pool with an exclusion set, and writing shows the learner every prompt. This is a code fix, not a content fix, so it is flagged rather than applied.

3. Reading steps up once, then goes backwards

A1 through B1 is 100% literal-detail retrieval — zero inference, gist, purpose or tone at any of those three levels, so B1 is no harder than A1 except in vocabulary. A real step up happens at B2. Then it stops: B2 and C1 have identical skill splits, and C2 regresses to become the most literal band in the whole corpus.

Two alarms from the previous session that were false

Listening audio is fully generated

The earlier session reported that 162 listening questions and 102 passages had no audio and were falling back to robotic browser speech, with an action item for you to log into production and re-run the generator ten times. That was wrong. Every standalone listening question (168/168) and every listening passage (54/54) has a real generated audio file, and there are zero browser-TTS fallback rows. The 162 "missing" are passage-linked questions, which are never meant to carry their own audio — mock-exam.tsx:492 resolves passage ? passage.audio_url : current.audio_url. The "102 passages" figure counted reading and grammar passages, which are read, not heard. No admin action is needed.

The writing word-count format is not broken

A critical was predicted on the theory that paraphrased word-count ranges would break the live word counter. The regex at practice.writing.tsx:45 is /\((\d+)\s*[–-]\s*(\d+)\s*words?\)/i — the character class accepts both en-dash and hyphen, and all 72 writing prompts match it. The reviewer reported it could not manufacture a finding honestly. The invariant was re-checked after every repair: still 72/72.

The verifiers were more valuable for catching bad fixes than bad findings

Fifteen reviewer-proposed repairs would have reintroduced or created a defect, and were rejected or rewritten. A passage rewrite that would have destroyed the evidence for a sibling question. A replacement whose key was an unsupported inference — worse than the guessable item it replaced. A replacement distractor (irrevocable) that collided with its own item's key. An explanation teaching "He will soon come back" when the item's actual answer is "...come back from lunch soon".

A sixteenth was caught by simulating the whole fix set before writing any SQL: a quoted fragment used as a whole-prompt replacement would have dropped a (NN–NN words) range and broken the live word counter — the very defect this audit was checking for. Writing and speaking repairs were switched to token-level edits as a result.

Method

Mechanical sweep. All 1,086 gradeable items checked programmatically for keys missing from options, duplicate options, gap-fill canonical answers missing from their accept-list, missing blank markers, and the reconstruction join invariant. Zero defects — re-verified independently this session rather than taken on trust. A further referential check of all 108 word_bank items (answer present in pool, ordinal matching a real blank, question count matching blank count) also came back clean.

Review. Twelve reviewers, one per skill and level band, each reading actual items against their passages. Two that died on session limits in the previous run were re-run here.

Verification. All 107 criticals and majors given to independent verifiers briefed to refute them and to re-test every proposed fix against the defect it removes.

Repair. Written to a resumable manifest first, then applied as a single transactional migration. The complete fix set was simulated in memory and all seven invariants re-checked before any SQL was generated — which is how the word-counter regression was caught. Invariants re-confirmed against the live database afterwards.

Gate. bun run build passes. bun run lint reports 979 pre-existing errors — all Prettier formatting in source files this audit never touched (no source file was modified; the repairs are database rows plus two new files). Lint was already red before this session.

Not covered. No learner-outcome data was available, so difficulty claims rest on item analysis rather than response statistics. Audio was not listened to; its presence and format were verified, not its quality.
